Local Flattening¶

This document plans a reimplementation, as MeshLab filters, of Pietroni, Corsini, Cignoni and Scopigno, An Interactive Local Flattening Operator to Support Digital Investigations on Artwork Surfaces (IEEE TVCG 17(12), Visualization 2011; .reference/papers/IEEEViz_local_flattening_final_v2.pdf). Not implemented; drafted 2026-11-13. The decisions marked open need Paolo, who co-authored the paper.

See also: Texture Signal Processing (per-face curvature directions and line-integral convolution, stage 4), Directional, TexMetro, Adding a Filter, Vocabulary, and the interactive tools plan in the interactive-tools-design notes.

What the paper does¶

A user sketches two polylines on a scanned sculpture: a guidance polyline, along which the patch runs, and an interval polyline, whose length is the patch’s half-width. The operator then:

  1. Selects the faces whose geodesic distance from the guidance polyline is below the interval’s length, and rejects the selection unless it is a disk.

  2. Interpolates a cross field over the selection that follows the guidance polyline: a discrete harmonic (cotangent) parametrization with a least-squares term pulling the polyline’s points to a constant U (eq. 1, stiffness k_st), the two ends of the polyline fixed; the U direction of that map, carried back to 3D, is the field.

  3. Parametrizes the selection aligned to the field, as the field-aligned methods do [Bommes et al. 2009], but on a disk, so with no cuts or integer variables.

  4. Crops to the rectangle spanned in UV by the ends of the two polylines.

On the resulting rectangular patch (Table 1: edge-length ratio between 0.83 and 1.17, average error under 5%):

  1. Height map of the residual marks (§4.1): the patch, built on a medium-resolution model (250k faces for the Pietà), is sampled into a geometry image at a chosen resolution (0.25 mm per pixel), smoothed by a Gaussian to keep only the overall shape, and each sample is cast along its normal onto the full-resolution scan; the distances form the image.

  2. Chisel traces (§4.2): ridges and valleys of that image at a scale, after Lindeberg 1998: in a Gaussian scale space L = I * G(σ), with p and q the image’s principal-curvature directions, ridges where L_p = 0, L_pp < 0, |L_pp| ≥ |L_qq|, valleys symmetrically; drawn colored by orientation, and grouped by similarity to a trace the user picks.

  3. Comparing photographs (§5): photos registered to the model before and after a restoration are projected onto the same patch, giving two images of the same rectangle of surface.

  4. Marks against curvature (§6): the principal curvature directions of a smoothed low-resolution model, drawn over the trace image.

Proposed stages¶

  1. The flattening filter. Parametrize Surface along Polyline (Local Flattening), name to be decided. Inputs: the current mesh, the guidance polyline (a mesh parameter naming a polyline layer), the patch’s half-width (absperc), and the cross-field construction if question 1 keeps both. Output: a new layer, the rectangular patch, with per-wedge UVs in the surface’s units (one UV unit is one mesh unit, so lengths read off the image), and the distortion figures as output values. Refuses a region that is not a disk. Mostly vcglib: geodesic, cross field, PoissonSolver, clipping. Tests: a cylinder (developable, so the patch must be isometric up to discretization), then a scan.

  2. The height map. A filter from the patch layer and the full-resolution layer to a raster layer holding a 16-bit height image at a given resolution, with the smoothing scale σ; the height range as output values. Tests: a known bump function added to a smooth surface must come back.

  3. Chisel traces. Ridges and valleys at a scale, as polyline layers: in the image’s space, or carried back onto the 3D surface through the patch, colored by orientation. Grouping by similarity to a user-picked trace is left for later.

  4. Workflows on existing filters: photos before and after a restoration, curvature directions against the traces; a page in the user documentation.

  5. The sketch tool. Drawing the two polylines on the surface, as the paper does, built as a front end that runs stage 1’s filter (one gesture, one filter run, one undo step).

Open questions for Paolo¶

  1. The cross field. Eq. 1 pulls the polyline’s points to a constant U, which maps the polyline to a line parallel to the V axis, while the text says the patch is aligned with U. Which was meant, and was the field the gradient of U or of V? Would seeding the faces the polyline crosses with its tangent and propagating with CrossField give the same patches in practice? It needs no new solver.

  2. PoissonSolver. Is vcglib’s the solver of the paper? It fixes one vertex when following a field and two otherwise; was the patch’s scale left to the field (unit-length directions, so lengths come out isometric), as fieldScale suggests?

  3. The interval polyline. In a filter, a width is simpler than a second polyline. Was the interval ever used for anything but its length?

  4. Which model for which step. The paper parametrizes a medium-resolution model and samples the full-resolution one. Two layers in MeshLab, or one layer simplified on the fly?

  5. Image processing. Ridges and valleys need Gaussian derivatives on images, which MeshLab has nowhere. Write the small separable convolution needed, or bring in an image library? Is OpenCV acceptable as an optional dependency?

  6. Data. The paper used the Pietà di Palestrina, the St Matthew and the David. Are any of those scans available for tests? .reference/2007Delos_Afrodite holds 1 GB of range maps of a sculpture, an alternative.
