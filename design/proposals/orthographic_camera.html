Orthographic Camera¶

This document proposes an orthographic projection for the 3D view and everything that consumes a camera. All five stages of Staging are implemented – the vcglib matrix fix; the view itself (state, JSON, projection, navigation, rays, lighting); and cameras as data (camera glyphs, snapshot and raster cameras, camera and raster filters); and occlusion (visible-only rectangle selection casts parallel rays) – and an orthographic state can be set through the camera JSON, the Python API or the camera filters, and from the View menu, the 5 key and a preference (stage 5). What remains is listed in Open questions.

See also: Rendering (the QRhi pipeline, camera models, the render-state JSON), Data Model (what an undo state holds), Architecture.

Decisions¶

Taken 2026-10-11 and binding on what follows:

  1. Orthographic only. No isometric, Cavalieri or other oblique camera types. The two enumerators that name them in CameraShot::CameraType are removed.

  2. The JSON is extended, and orthographic works everywhere – on screen, in snapshots, in every programmatic rendering and in every filter that reads a camera. A feature that cannot handle an orthographic camera is a defect of this work, not a documented limitation.

  3. The scale is stored so that switching projection is seamless. The visible height at the orbit centre is the one quantity both projections share, and it is already what the trackball is built on (see The invariant).

Where things stand¶

The camera is ViewTrackball: an orbit centre, a rotation, a distance, a radius and a vertical field of view clamped to 10-120 degrees. projectionMatrix() calls perspective() and nothing else. The trackball JSON payload, the undo ViewState, the view synchronization and the render-state JSON all carry that one state, and none of them names a projection.

A second camera model exists for raster layers, CameraShot, which wraps vcg::Shot. vcglib knows an ORTHO camera type, and Camera::Project and UnProject honour it, but Camera::GetMatrix – the function that builds the matrix the GPU path uses – always builds a perspective matrix, whatever the type (vcglib/vcg/math/camera.ipp:144). The GL wrapper in the same library calls glOrtho for ORTHO (wrap/gl/camera.h:163), so the library disagrees with itself.

Much of the pipeline is already projection-agnostic because it goes through a matrix: the depth pick unprojects through the inverse of the view-projection, the rectangle selection projects through it, and the transform and measure tools unproject a one-pixel offset. Those need no change.

The invariant¶

Let H = 2 * distance * tan(fovY / 2): the world-space height visible at the orbit centre. In a perspective view it is what the camera frames; in an orthographic view it is the height of the view volume.

Orthographic projection uses exactly that H. Consequences:

  • Switching is seamless. The orbit centre, and everything on the plane through it, keeps its size and position on screen. Nothing is recomputed when toggling, and toggling back restores the perspective view exactly, because distance and fovYDeg are kept in both modes.

  • A small field of view is the limit of the orthographic view. As fovY shrinks with H held constant, distance grows and the perspective image converges on the orthographic one. The two ends of the range are the same family, which is also what makes a probe for the implementation possible (see Tests).

  • The navigation arithmetic does not change. Pan converts pixels to world units with distance * tan(fovY/2), the wheel scales distance, the trackball gizmo’s radius scales with distance * tan(fovY/2), and every framing routine (reset, centre on selection, canonical views) computes distance from a radius and a field of view. All of them are statements about H, so all of them stay correct.

What does change is the eye. In perspective the eye is a point and rays fan out from it; in orthographic the eye position only fixes where depth is measured from, and rays are parallel. Everything below is a consequence of that.

The camera state¶

ViewTrackball::State gains one field, projection, an enum with Perspective (the default) and Orthographic. distance, fovYDeg, radius and the near-clip ratio keep their meaning.

  • In orthographic, the wheel still changes distance, so it still zooms, by changing H.

  • Shift+wheel is the way in and out. It is the dolly-zoom that trades field of view for distance at constant H: forward narrows the field of view, flattening the perspective, and orthographic is the limit of that. It therefore does not stop at the narrowest field of view (10 degrees); it goes on into orthographic, at that field of view. Turned back, it leaves orthographic for perspective at the same field of view and widens from there. Forward again in orthographic does nothing – nothing is flatter than parallel. H is untouched throughout, so nothing jumps at the switch.

  • The projection matrix is ortho(-W/2, W/2, -H/2, H/2, near, far) with W = H * aspect. The depth range is fitted to the scene: the view sets the world-space box of everything visible each frame (setSceneBounds), and the volume runs from just before the nearest corner of that box, as a distance in front of the eye, to just after the farthest, widened to hold the trackball gizmo about the orbit centre (which a small box would not cover). The slack is 5% of the range, so surfaces on the box faces, wide lines and the tips of normals are not cut, plus a thousandth of the view height for a box that is flat against the view. near may be negative: with the orbit centre closer to the eye than the scene is deep, an orthographic view still draws what is behind the eye. Without bounds – before the first frame, or a camera rebuilt from JSON, where only x and y matter – it falls back to a symmetric reach of 4 * max(radius, sceneRadius) about the centre. Perspective keeps near = distance * nearClipRatio and its generous far plane.

  • The depth range follows the backend. The renderer hands QRhi the projection matrix uncorrected, with depth in [-1, 1], and the pick reads depth back as ndcZ = depth01 on a backend that clips depth to [0, 1] (Metal, Direct3D, Vulkan). A perspective matrix survives that because its depth is nonlinear: it loses only the sliver in front of twice the near distance. An orthographic matrix has linear depth, so a volume mapped to [-1, 1] would lose its whole nearer half – the front of the object clipped away at the moment of switching. ViewTrackball::setClipDepthZeroToOne, set by the view from QRhi::isClipDepthZeroToOne(), makes the orthographic matrix map its volume to the range the backend keeps; the ray origin, and so the pick’s unprojection, follow. Perspective is deliberately left as it was.

  • nearClipRatio is a perspective depth-precision setting and is ignored in orthographic, where depth is linear over [near, far].

  • projectionMatrix(aspect, minFarDistance, maxNearDistance): a peer view’s camera glyph can reach past the host’s far plane, and in orthographic it can lie behind the eye. viewFrustumFarDistance() and viewFrustumNearDistance() (the nearest corner of the glyphs’ bounds, negative behind the eye) widen the volume to hold them; perspective ignores the near request.

JSON¶

The trackball payload, shared by the camera JSON (MeshLab.CameraState), the render-state JSON (MeshLab.RenderState) and the PNG metadata (MeshLab.CameraTrackballState), gains

"projection": "orthographic"

The writer omits it when it is "perspective", as it omits every default-valued field. The reader treats an absent key as perspective, accepts exactly the two strings, and fails with a message naming the key for anything else.

The change is additive: kind and version stay as they are, as nothing checks version today. A build that predates this work reading an orthographic file would ignore the key and render it in perspective, silently. That is the cost of an additive change; it is worth stating in the Python API documentation and nowhere else.

Everything that already round-trips the trackball state inherits the field with no further work: the undo ViewState, view splitting and synchronization, the camera copy/paste, RenderWidget::cameraStateJson() and applyCameraStateJson(), the camera_state filter parameter, and the Python camera_state_json. The one place that must be touched by hand is the equality test fuzzyStateEqual (renderwidget.cpp:196), which lists the fields one by one and would otherwise miss a projection change.

Rays¶

A ray through a pixel is generated by the trackball (viewRayFromWindow), by the CameraShot of a raster layer, by the select filter, and implicitly by every filter that treats viewPoint() - p as the direction to the camera. Each assumes the origin is the eye. This work gives them one answer.

ViewRay { origin, direction }, world space, direction a unit vector.

  • ViewTrackball::viewRay(pos, viewport): perspective, origin is the eye and the direction passes through the pixel; orthographic, the direction is the view direction and the origin is the pixel unprojected onto the near plane. hitViewPlane, hitHyper and hitSphereLikeVcg use it instead of viewRayFromWindow; their formulas are written in terms of distance and stay valid under the invariant, so the rotation feel is the same across a toggle.

  • CameraShot::viewRayThrough(pixel) and CameraShot::directionToCamera(worldPoint): the unit vector from a point towards the camera – normalize(viewPoint - p) in perspective, -viewDirection in orthographic. It is what the raster filters need; the three call sites that write viewPoint() - p today (filter_camera, filter_color_projection) switch to it.

  • Occlusion gets a direction form, ViewpointOccluder::isOccludedAlong(sample, towardViewer), alongside the existing isOccluded(sample, eye). select_by_rectangle with visible only picks the form from the camera’s projection; for orthographic the direction is carried into the layer’s local space with the inverse of the layer matrix. Both backends already answer a bounded query – Embree a segment (segmentUnoccluded), the vcglib grid a ray whose hit distance is compared with the sample’s – so the direction form is the same query with its far end placed at the layer’s bounding-box diagonal beyond the sample, which is outside the layer by construction.

The depth pick, the rectangle test, the transform tool (which unprojects a one-pixel offset) and the measure tool (which projects points through the view-projection) already work for either projection and are not touched.

Shaders¶

Four fragment shaders light with the vector to the eye taken as -viewPos (fill_flat.frag:126, fill_smooth.frag:129 as the specular half-vector’s V; fill_wire_overlay.frag:32 and overlay_points.frag:41 as the light direction). In orthographic that vector is the constant (0, 0, 1).

The shaders need to know which projection is in use. lightDir.w, a component of the main uniform block that was written as 0 and read by nothing, carries it: 0 for perspective, 1 for orthographic. It is read from the projection matrix the frame is drawn with (isOrthographicProjection: a last row of (0, 0, 0, 1)), not from the trackball, so it holds for whichever camera built the matrix – a raster’s included, once rasters are handled. overlay_points.frag, whose block declaration stops short of lightDir, extends it. No binding or buffer layout changes.

Unchanged, and worth recording why:

  • Object-space point size projects an offset of the requested length and reads w from the result; in orthographic w is 1 and it gives the plain scale.

  • The trackball gizmo’s back-hemisphere shading uses the direction from the orbit centre to the eye, which is the view axis in both projections.

  • Radiance scaling stores log(-viewZ). It keeps working, but its parameters were tuned against a perspective depth where the logarithm normalizes for distance; in orthographic its strength follows the arbitrary distance. This is the one place the result may look different rather than wrong; see Open questions.

Camera glyphs¶

Both glyphs draw a pyramid: four edges from an apex to the corners of the far rectangle, plus the near and far rectangles.

Implemented (stage 3). What follows is the design; the implementation matches it, with the box drawn from the near rectangle (which may sit behind the eye) to the far one.

  • A peer view’s camera (buildViewFrustumGizmo) reads cot(fovY/2) out of proj(1,1), which for an orthographic matrix is 2/H and means nothing as a cotangent. It must recognize an orthographic matrix – its last row is (0, 0, 0, 1) – and draw a box: twelve segments, the four depth edges parallel, no apex. PeerViewCamera already carries the matrix, so nothing else is passed.

  • A raster layer’s camera (renderwidget_raster.cpp) builds its glyph from an apex and four unprojected corners. For an orthographic shot the apex lines become the four parallel edges of a box from the plane through the viewpoint to the glyph depth; the glyph’s vertex buffer holds up to 12 segments and the draw count follows the shape (8 for a pyramid).

Cameras as data¶

CameraShot is the camera of a raster layer and the form a view takes when it is exported – a snapshot added as a raster, cameraShotForViewport(), the Render from Render-State JSON filter’s output, HeadlessRenderContext::cameraShot().

  • CameraShot::CameraType is reduced to Perspective and Orthographic. fromVcgShot maps vcglib’s ISOMETRIC and CAVALIERI to Perspective, and the project loader, which knows the file, logs a warning naming it: nothing downstream can honour those types, and they come only from older projects.

  • cameraShotForViewport() (renderwidget.cpp:1380) hard-codes PERSPECTIVE. For an orthographic trackball it builds an ORTHO shot whose frustum is the view volume: ViewportPx the requested size, CenterPx its middle, PixelSizeMm the world units per pixel (H / height), and FocalMm unused. Project, UnProject and Depth already treat ORTHO correctly, so a snapshot added as a raster layer projects onto the mesh in the same place the view drew it.

  • Implementation notes (stage 3).

    • cameraShotForViewport() also moves an orthographic shot’s viewpoint back along the axis until the whole scene is in front of it. Parallel rays do not care where the camera sits, but depth buffers and the projection filters read depth <= 0 as “behind the camera”, and the orbit eye can lie inside the scene. The image is unchanged.

    • A raster whose shot is orthographic draws in raster mode with a volume fitted to the scene (a little slack each end, the near plane free to be behind the viewpoint), and the same [-1, 1] to [0, 1] depth fold the trackball uses on a zero-to-one backend (ViewTrackball::depthFoldToZeroToOne). CameraShot::projectionMatrix accepts a near plane at or behind the viewpoint for an orthographic shot.

    • The framing filters set_camera_from_direction and set_camera_to_view_selection, and set_raster_camera, take a projection parameter (Keep current, Perspective, Orthographic; default Keep current, which reads the current raster’s camera and is perspective without one). The visible height at the target is the same either way. rotate_cameras, translate_cameras and scale_cameras keep the intrinsics, and RescalingWorld scales an orthographic shot’s PixelSizeMm, which is world units per pixel, correctly.

    • The loaders (document_internal.cpp, layerfilterplugin.cpp) read camera type 2 or 3 as perspective and log a warning; ImgPatchParam’s orientation weight, the soft depth buffer’s front-facing test and the colour projection filters use directionToCamera.

  • vcglib, two changes in vcg/math/camera.ipp, the only work outside this repository. It follows the four-part procedure (commit in vcglib/, push to origin/devel, bump the gitlink, land the dependent MeshLab code in the same bump). GetMatrix has exactly one caller in the tree, CameraShot::projectionMatrix().

    • GetMatrix gets the missing orthographic branch. For an ORTHO camera it builds the glOrtho matrix from the frustum extents – 2/(r-l), 2/(t-b), -2/(f-n) on the diagonal, the translations in the last column, a last row of (0, 0, 0, 1) – with the same clip-space convention as the perspective branch. Today it builds the perspective matrix whatever the type: for an ORTHO camera the GPU path disagrees with Shot::Project by hundreds of pixels (measured: 399 px worst case over random points).

    • GetFrustum has the horizontal extent mirrored. Found while measuring the first change: SetFrustum(left -1, right 3, ...) followed by GetFrustum returns left -3, right 1. The vertical extents round-trip; the horizontal ones do not. GetFrustum returns right = cx * p and left = -(W - cx) * p, where Project and SetFrustum both mean left = -cx * p and right = (W - cx) * p (cx the principal point in pixels, p the pixel size, W the width). The two agree only when the principal point is centred, which is why nothing shows it on a synthetic camera. With an off-centre principal point – every calibrated photogrammetry camera – the existing perspective matrix places the optical axis at pixel W - cx instead of cx: an error of 2 * |W/2 - cx| pixels, in x only. The fix is the same two lines in GetFrustum, and it corrects GetMatrix and the GL wrapper (wrap/gl/camera.h) for every user at once. It is a change of behaviour for off-centre cameras and must be said so in the commit message.

    With both changes, GetMatrix followed by the view matrix agrees with Shot::Project to under 1e-3 pixel for random points, for both camera types and for centred and off-centre principal points (measured on a scratch copy of the functions). CameraShot::projectionMatrix() then needs no change, and the raster overlay and the projected-raster pass, which both call it, become correct for an orthographic raster – and for an off-centre perspective one, which they are not today.

  • The camera filters (filter_camera) write a trackball payload and a shot with PERSPECTIVE hard-coded (camerafilterplugin.cpp:633, :716). They keep the projection of the camera they start from, and Set Camera from Direction and Set Camera to View Selection gain a projection parameter whose default is “current”.

Interface¶

Added last, so that nothing can be switched on before everything works:

  • View menu, “Orthographic Projection”, checkable and per view, bound to the 5 key (the key Blender users expect, and the one Marco Callieri asked for). It sets State::projection and nothing else.

  • The on-view readout that says FOV: 45.0 deg says Orthographic instead.

  • Preference view.projection (perspective | orthographic, default perspective): the projection a new or reset view starts in, beside view.fieldOfView. Reset camera keeps the current projection. Implemented (stage 5). Differences from the sketch above: the 5 binding is attached only while a 3D view has focus (MainWindow::updateViewShortcuts, like Ctrl+C), because a bare digit would otherwise flip the projection while typing into a spin box or the console; the menu item is disabled outside 3D mode; RenderWidget::setProjection also shows a status line (“Orthographic projection” / “Perspective projection”); ViewTrackball::resetToFrame leaves the projection alone, so Reset Camera keeps it, and the preference is read once, when a view is created. The on-view readout is the Shift+wheel one from stage 2 (“FOV: orthographic”); there is no permanent readout.

  • Canonical views (the numeric-keypad views of the same request) need nothing beyond this: snapCameraToAxis keeps the field of view and the projection, and a canonical view in orthographic is the usual way to read one.

  • The quick-help overlay and the Python API documentation describe the new key and the new JSON field.

Staging¶

The interface (row 42) lands last in every stage plan, so that no stage leaves a reachable orthographic view that some consumer cannot draw.

  1. vcglib. Done. Row 35 alone, with a test in this repository (vcglib has no test suite of its own) that CameraShot::projectionMatrix() through the view matrix agrees with CameraShot::project() for both camera types and for centred and off-centre principal points. Independent of everything else and landed by the four-part procedure; the off-centre correction is worth reporting to whoever uses raster overlays before it lands, since it moves them.

  2. The view. Done. Rows 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15, 21, 22, 26. An orthographic state, set by JSON or the Python API, renders, navigates and lights correctly in a view, a snapshot and a headless render; there is no menu item yet.

  3. Cameras as data. Done. Rows 27, 28, 30, 34, 37, 38, 39 (and 36 and 40 follow from stage 1). Peer and raster camera glyphs, snapshot-to-raster, the camera filters and the raster filters. The probe tests/render_probes/orthographic also renders an orthographic view into a raster and projects it back onto the sphere, including a camera whose orbit eye is inside the sphere.

  4. Occlusion. Done. Row 18. ViewpointOccluder::isOccludedAlong is isOccluded with the far end of the segment a bounding-box diagonal (plus 10%) beyond the sample, so both backends needed no new query. select_by_rectangle picks the form from the camera’s projection and carries the view direction into layer space with mapVector of the inverse layer matrix. The test puts the orbit eye between two cubes of one layer, where the eye rule and the direction rule disagree about the upper cube.

  5. Interface and documentation. Done. Rows 42, 43, 44: View > Orthographic Projection (5), preference view.projection, quick help, python_scripting.md, rendering.md.

Tests¶

ViewTrackball had no unit test, so stage 2 added MeshLab2ViewTrackballTests, and the render probe tests/render_probes/orthographic (a sphere at constant H: orthographic coverage independent of the field of view, perspective not, perspective at the smallest field of view close to orthographic, a 2:1 frame at the same scale).

  • The invariant. For random states, a point on the plane through the orbit centre projects to the same normalized coordinates in both projections; a point H/2 above the centre projects to y = 1 in orthographic at any distance.

  • Rays. Perspective rays through a state share one origin; orthographic rays share one direction and their origins lie on the near plane; project(unproject(p)) == p for both, through CameraShot as well.

  • JSON. Round trip; perspective omitted from the output; an absent key reads as perspective; an unknown value fails naming the key.

  • Glyphs. buildViewFrustumGizmo on an orthographic matrix gives twelve segments with the four depth edges parallel, and a near plane behind the eye is accepted (stage 3, test_viewfrustumgizmo.cpp).

  • Shots and filters (stage 3). DocumentTests::cameraShotRaysAndDirectionsFollowTheProjection (ray through a pixel hits the point that projects there; parallel rays and a constant direction to the camera for ORTHO), cameraShotReadsUnsupportedTypesAsPerspective, and FilterTests::cameraFramingFiltersMakeAndKeepOrthographicShots (Keep current keeps, an explicit projection overrides).

  • vcglib. Through CameraShot: the projection matrix against project() and depth() for ORTHO and PERSPECTIVE, with the principal point centred and off-centre in x and in y; and the SetFrustum / GetFrustum round trip.

  • Selection. select_by_rectangle with visible only in orthographic selects the face toward the viewer and not the one behind it.

  • Raster filters. A normal orientation against an orthographic raster camera uses the view direction, not the direction to the camera’s position.

  • Render probe (tests/render_probes/orthographic, through the application’s own --generate-docs hook like the existing probes): a unit cube drawn through the render-state JSON in both projections. In orthographic the silhouette of the front-facing cube is a square, with the back face hidden behind it; in perspective it is not. A perspective render at the minimum field of view with H held constant differs from the orthographic one by less than a stated bound, which is the small-field-of-view limit of the invariant measured. A lit plane facing the camera has a uniform specular term in orthographic and a falling one in perspective. The same JSON through HeadlessRenderContext gives the same image.

Open questions¶

  • Radiance scaling. Its parameters follow distance in orthographic. Normalizing by H instead would make it independent of zoom; measure on a real scan before deciding, and leave it alone if the look is acceptable.

  • Near and far when zoomed far in. With distance much smaller than the scene the orthographic near is deeply negative. It is correct, and the fit keeps it as tight as the scene allows; check that nothing drawn outside the scene box – a tool overlay, a decorator – is cut by the 5% slack on a real scan.

  • isOccludedAlong and grazing rays. The tolerance the grid backend applies is proportional to the sample’s distance (dist * 1e-3); with the far end placed one bounding-box diagonal away that tolerance is larger than for a perspective eye close to the layer. Measure the false-negative rate on a real scan, and scale the tolerance with the layer rather than with the query if it is too loose.
